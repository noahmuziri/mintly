Inflation: why the same money buys less

About 12 minutes · 2 calculators · Your answers are saved in this browser

Quick story

Ask a parent or grandparent what your favorite snack cost when they were your age. It was almost certainly less. Same snack, higher price. That's inflation, and it's quietly shrinking every dollar you save.

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. When prices go up, each dollar buys a little less than it used to. It's slow enough that you rarely notice it day to day, but over years it adds up to a lot.

Why prices rise

  • Costs go up. If wages, ingredients, shipping or rent cost a business more, it usually passes some of that on.
  • Demand outruns supply. When lots of people want something and there isn't enough of it, sellers can charge more.
  • More money chasing the same goods. When a lot of new money enters the economy faster than the amount of stuff to buy, prices tend to climb.

In the United States, prices have often risen around 2% to 3% a year over long stretches, but some years have been much higher or lower. Check a current source for today's number.

What it does to savings

If your savings earn less than inflation, you're losing buying power even though the number in your account goes up. What matters is the real return: the interest you earn minus inflation.

What you can do about it

  • Don't let big, long-term money sit idle. Money you won't need for many years usually needs to grow faster than inflation, which means taking on some risk (Lesson 5).
  • Keep emergency money safe and accessible. It's fine for it to just hold its value, because its job is to be there.
  • Grow your income. Skills and raises that beat inflation make you better off.
  • Notice it. Compare prices over time and ask whether a "deal" is still a deal.
