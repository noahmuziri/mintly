Scams, fraud and staying safe

About 12 minutes · 2 activities · Your answers are saved in this browser

Scammers target teens because teens are online a lot, often have their own money or accounts, and may not have been warned yet. The good news is that nearly every scam relies on the same few tricks. Learn the tricks and you can spot almost all of them.

The red flags

  • Pressure. "Act now or lose it." Real offers and real banks give you time to think.
  • A request for secrets. Passwords, PINs and one-time codes are never something a real bank, game or company will ask you to send.
  • Strange payment methods. Gift cards, crypto and wire transfers are hard to reverse, which is why scammers love them.
  • Too good to be true. Free money, guaranteed returns, or a prize for a contest you never entered.
  • Secrecy. "Don't tell your parents." A legitimate person won't ask you to hide it.
  • An unexpected message with a link. Especially one that creates fear or excitement.

Common scams aimed at teens

  • Phishing: a fake text, email or DM that looks like your bank, a delivery company or a game, trying to get you to enter your login on a fake website.
  • Fake giveaways and "free" game currency: you "win" something, then must pay a fee or share your account.
  • Overpayment and "easy job" scams: someone sends a check, asks you to send some back, and the check later bounces.
  • Account takeovers: a friend's hacked account asks for money or a code.
  • Get-rich-quick investing: "guaranteed" returns, crypto "signals", or someone promising to flip your money.
Rule of thumb If a message makes your heart race, slow down. Scammers want you to feel rushed, because rushed people don't check.

What to do: stop, check, tell

  • Stop. Don't click, reply, or send money or codes.
  • Check. Go to the real website by typing it yourself, or call the real number on your card. Don't use the link or number in the message.
  • Tell. Talk to a parent, teacher or the bank. Reporting helps others, and there's no shame in being targeted. Scams fool smart adults every day.

Protect your accounts

  • Use a different, long password for each important account. A password manager helps.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication for email, banking and games.
  • Keep your phone and apps updated, and don't log in on links you didn't ask for.
  • If something goes wrong, tell someone right away. Speed matters.

Key words from this lesson

Scam
A trick to take your money or information.
Phishing
A fake message that tries to steal your login or details.
Two-factor authentication
A second step, like a code, that protects your account.
Red flag
A warning sign that something may be a scam.
Identity theft
Someone using your personal information without permission.
