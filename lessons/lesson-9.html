Financial Literacy · Lesson 9

Scams and staying safe with money

About 12 minutes · 4 activities · Your answers and progress are saved to your account

Scammers love teenagers: you're online all day, you're getting your first money, and you may not have been taught what to watch for. The good news is that nearly every scam uses the same handful of tricks. Learn them once and you'll spot them everywhere.

Warm-up · Typing

What would make you trust a message about money?

Someone you don't know messages you offering a way to earn money fast. What would make you believe it, and what would make you suspicious?

The scams teens actually meet

  • Phishing: a text, email or DM that looks like it's from a bank, delivery company or game, and tries to get you to click a link and enter your login.
  • Fake giveaways and "free" game currency: you only have to share your password or "verify" with a code.
  • Fake jobs and overpayment scams: someone sends you a cheque or payment, asks you to keep part and send the rest back, and later the original payment bounces, leaving you out of pocket.
  • Money mules: being asked to receive money into your account and pass it on, often for a "cut." This can be illegal even if you didn't know.
  • Investment scams: "guaranteed" returns, secret tips, and influencers who profit when you buy. A pump and dump is when people hype a cheap asset so the price jumps, then sell as everyone else buys.
  • Marketplace scams: tickets, sneakers or consoles that are "sold" and never arrive.

The red flags

Red flag (noun)

A warning sign that something may be a scam. One red flag is a reason to stop. Several means walk away.

  • Pressure and urgency: "act now," "last chance," "your account will be closed."
  • Secrecy: "don't tell your parents," "keep this between us."
  • Strange payment methods: gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers or sending money to a "friend." Real businesses don't demand these.
  • Guaranteed returns: no real investment can guarantee a profit. Anyone who promises one is lying or doesn't understand risk.
  • Asking for secrets: your password, PIN, or the six-digit code texted to you. No real company asks for these.
  • Too good to be true: big money for little effort.

Pause, verify, tell

  1. Pause. Scammers want you rushed. Take a breath, and wait before you click or pay.
  2. Verify. Don't use the link or number in the message. Open the official app or website yourself, or call the number on your card.
  3. Tell. Tell a parent or another adult you trust, even if you're embarrassed. Everyone gets fooled sometimes; telling quickly is what limits the damage.

Protect your accounts

  • Use a different, long password for each important account. A password manager helps.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA): a second step, like a code, after your password.
  • Keep personal details private: full birthday, address, ID numbers, and bank details.

If you think you've been scammed: stop paying, tell an adult, contact your bank, change your passwords, and report it. In the US you can report at reportfraud.ftc.gov; other countries have similar reporting sites.

Key words from this lesson

Phishing
Fake messages that try to steal personal or login details.
Red flag
A warning sign of a scam.
Pump and dump
Hyping a cheap asset, then selling to those who buy in.
Money mule
A person used to move money for scammers.
Two-factor authentication (2FA)
A second step after your password to prove it's you.
Overpayment scam
A fake payment bigger than agreed, with a request to send back the difference.
