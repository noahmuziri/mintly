Compound interest and the power of time

About 14 minutes · 2 activities · Your answers are saved in this browser

Imagine a snowball rolling down a hill. It starts small, but as it rolls it picks up snow, gets bigger, and picks up even more snow because it has a bigger surface. Compound interest works the same way: your money earns interest, and then that interest earns interest too.

This is the quiet force behind most long-term wealth, and it's also why debt can grow so fast. The good news for you: it rewards time, and you have a lot of it.

Simple vs. compound interest

  • Simple interest is paid only on the money you started with. $1,000 at 7% earns $70 every year, no matter how long you wait.
  • Compound interest is paid on the money you started with and on the interest you've already earned. In year two you earn 7% of $1,070, not $1,000.

The gap looks tiny at first and then becomes huge. Over 30 years, $1,000 at 7% would grow to about $3,100 with simple interest, but about $7,600 with compound interest. (This uses a steady 7% for illustration. Real returns go up and down and aren't guaranteed.)

Compound interest (noun)

Interest earned on both your original money and on the interest it has already earned, so growth speeds up over time.

The Rule of 72

Here's a handy shortcut. To estimate how many years it takes for money to double, divide 72 by the yearly return. At 7% a year, 72 ÷ 7 is about 10 years. At 4%, it's 18 years. At 10%, about 7 years.

Why starting early beats starting big

Say Sam starts putting $50 a month away at age 15, and Alex starts the same $50 a month at 25. If both earn a steady 7% until age 65, Sam ends up with about $272,000 and Alex about $131,000. Same monthly amount. The extra ten years made Sam's total about twice as large.

Leaf asks What's one thing you'd have to skip each month to put away $50? Is a year of that thing worth more than the growth over 50 years? There's no right answer, just a trade-off worth noticing.

The same math works against you

Compound interest is exactly what makes credit card balances snowball. The rate that grows your savings also grows what you owe. Two rules follow: let interest work for you by saving and investing early, and don't let it work against you by avoiding high-interest debt.

Keep it realistic

Real investments don't earn a smooth 7% each year. Some years are up, some are down, and they can lose value. The lesson isn't a promise of a number. It's that time and consistency are powerful, and that costs and fees quietly eat into growth.
