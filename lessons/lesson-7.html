Financial Literacy · Lesson 7

Credit and debt: borrowing without getting stuck

About 14 minutes · 5 activities · Your answers and progress are saved to your account

Borrowing can help you buy a home or pay for school. It can also trap you for years in payments on something you used up long ago. The difference comes down to a few ideas you can learn right now: what interest really costs, how credit cards work, and why a number called a credit score follows you around.

Warm-up · Typing

Think about a time someone borrowed money, or you did. Was it a good idea? Write what you think and why.

Credit and debt

Credit is the ability to borrow money or buy now and pay later. Debt is the money you owe once you do. Borrowing always costs something extra: interest, the price of using someone else's money (you met it in Lesson 4, from the saver's side).

APR (noun)

Annual percentage rate: the yearly cost of borrowing, as a percentage. A higher APR means a more expensive loan. Always ask for the APR before you borrow.

How a credit card works

A credit card lets you spend the bank's money now and pay it back later. Each month you get a statement showing what you owe. Then you have a choice:

  • Pay the full statement balance by the due date, and in most cases you pay no interest at all.
  • Pay less (the minimum payment is the smallest amount allowed) and the rest starts costing interest, often 20% a year or more.

The minimum payment is the trap. It keeps the bank happy while your balance hangs around and interest keeps adding up. Use the calculator below to see how long.

Good debt, costly debt

  • Debt that can build your future: a student loan for a degree that raises your earnings, or a mortgage for a home. Still costs interest, so borrow carefully.
  • Debt that costs a lot for little: carrying a credit card balance, payday loans, and borrowing for things that lose value fast.
  • Buy now, pay later (BNPL): splitting a purchase into instalments. It can feel painless, which is exactly why people overspend. Late fees and missed payments add up, and a pile of small plans is still a pile of debt.

Your credit score

A credit score is a number, in the US usually between 300 and 850, that shows lenders how reliably you repay. A higher score means lower interest rates, and it can affect renting an apartment or getting some jobs. A typical score looks at:

  • Payment history (about 35%): do you pay on time? The biggest factor.
  • Amounts owed (about 30%): how much of your available credit you use.
  • Length of history (about 15%): how long you've had credit.
  • New credit (about 10%) and mix (about 10%).

In the US, you generally need to be 21 to open a credit card on your own, or have a parent as cosigner (or show your own income). Until then, the best preparation is habits: pay bills on time and never spend money you don't have.

Key words from this lesson

Credit / debt
Ability to borrow, and the money you owe once you do.
APR
The yearly cost of borrowing as a percentage.
Minimum payment
The smallest amount you can pay without being late.
Statement balance
What you owe at the end of the billing period.
Credit score
A number showing how reliably you repay debt.
Cosigner
Someone who agrees to repay if you can't.
BNPL
Buy now, pay later: splitting a purchase into instalments.
