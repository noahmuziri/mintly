Credit, debt and borrowing

About 14 minutes · 2 activities · Your answers are saved in this browser

Sooner or later you'll want something that costs more than you have: a phone, a car, a degree, a first apartment. Borrowing lets you use someone else's money now and pay it back later. It's not free, though. The price you pay for the loan is called interest, so borrowing is really renting money.

This lesson shows what borrowing costs, how to tell helpful debt from harmful debt, and how a credit score works.

Debt, interest and APR

APR (noun)

Annual percentage rate. The yearly cost of borrowing, shown as a percentage. A 20% APR means that, over a year, you'd pay about 20 cents in interest for every dollar you still owe.

When you compare loans, don't just look at the monthly payment. Ask three questions: What's the APR? How long will I be paying? What's the total I'll repay? A longer loan gives a smaller payment but usually costs more in total.

Good debt and bad debt

Debt isn't automatically bad. The question is whether what you're buying is likely to be worth more than the interest costs, and whether you can comfortably repay it.

  • Often worth considering: education that raises your future income, a mortgage on a home, a loan with a low rate and a clear repayment plan.
  • Usually not worth it: carrying a credit card balance month to month, buying wants on credit, and very high-interest loans like payday loans.

The minimum payment trap

Credit cards let you pay a small "minimum" each month. It feels affordable, but the rest of the balance keeps growing with interest. A single $500 purchase on a card with a high APR can take years to clear if you only pay the minimum, and cost far more than $500. The safe rule: pay the full balance every month, and if you can't, don't put it on the card.

Your credit score

A credit score is a number that tells lenders how reliably you repay. A higher score usually means lower interest rates, which can save thousands over a lifetime. In the US, scores run from 300 to 850, and these habits matter most:

  • Pay on time, every time. Payment history is the biggest single factor.
  • Use a small share of your limit. Staying under about 30% of your credit limit is a common guideline.
  • Keep accounts open a long time. A longer history helps.
  • Don't open lots of accounts at once. Each application can lower your score a little.

Under 18, you usually can't have your own credit card, but you can start good habits now: pay what you owe on time, even small amounts you owe friends and family.

Quick example Jo borrows $1,000 at 20% APR and repays it over 12 months. The payment is about $93 a month, so Jo repays about $1,112, which means about $112 of interest. If Jo had saved $93 a month first and bought it with cash, the extra $112 would still be in Jo's pocket.
