Financial Literacy · Lesson 6

Earning money: jobs, paychecks and taxes

About 12 minutes · 5 activities · Your answers and progress are saved to your account

Everything else in this course starts with money coming in. Whether it's an allowance, a babysitting job or your first real paycheck, there's one surprise almost everyone gets: the number you earn is not the number you take home. This lesson explains where the difference goes and how to read a pay stub with confidence.

Warm-up · Typing

What do you think you'd keep from a $100 paycheck?

Imagine you work a shift and your boss says you earned $100. How much do you think ends up in your pocket? Make a guess and say why.

Ways teens earn money

  • Allowance and chores: money from home for jobs around the house.
  • Informal work: babysitting, lawn care, pet sitting, tutoring. You set the price and agree the work.
  • A part-time job: you work for an employer who pays you on a schedule, such as every two weeks.
  • Making and selling: things you create or resell. If you earn regularly this way, ask a parent about whether taxes apply.

There are rules about when and how long people under 18 can work, and they vary by country and state. A parent, school counselor or your local labor department can tell you what applies to you.

Gross pay vs. net pay

Gross pay (noun)

What you earn before anything is taken out. If you work 10 hours at $12 an hour, your gross pay is $120.

Net pay (noun)

What you actually take home after taxes and other deductions come out. Also called take-home pay. This is the number to budget with.

Where the difference goes: taxes

Employers hold back, or withhold, taxes from each paycheck and send them to the government for you. In the United States, the usual ones are:

  • Payroll taxes (FICA): 7.65% of your pay, split into Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%). They help pay for retirement and health benefits.
  • Federal income tax: depends on how much you earn and on the form you fill in at the start of a job, the W-4. Teens who earn a small amount often owe little or none, and can get withheld money back by filing a tax return.
  • State and local taxes: these depend on where you live. Some places have none.

Taxes pay for things most people use every day: roads, schools, emergency services and public parks. Other countries have similar systems with different names and rates, so check what applies where you live.

Reading a pay stub

Each paycheck comes with a pay stub, a small record of how your pay was worked out. Check four things every time:

  • Hours worked and your hourly rate: do they match what you actually did?
  • Gross pay for the period.
  • Deductions: each tax, listed separately.
  • Net pay: the amount that reaches you.

Mistakes happen. If something looks wrong, ask your employer politely and keep your stubs; they're proof of what you earned.

Key words from this lesson

Gross pay
Pay before anything is taken out.
Net pay
Take-home pay, after taxes and deductions.
Withholding
Tax your employer holds back and sends to the government for you.
Payroll tax (FICA)
Social Security and Medicare taxes, 7.65% of pay in the US.
W-4
The form that tells your employer how much income tax to withhold.
Pay stub
A record of hours, pay, deductions and net pay for a pay period.
