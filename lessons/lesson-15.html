Paying for college and what student loans really cost

About 14 minutes · a calculator · Your answers are saved in this browser

Quick story

A degree might have a $100,000 sticker price. But almost nobody pays the sticker price. What matters is the net price, and how much of it you'd have to borrow. Two students, same degree, can end up owing very different amounts.

College, trade school or an apprenticeship can be a great investment in your future income. It can also be one of the biggest debts a young person ever takes on. This lesson is about making that choice with your eyes open. (Examples are US-based. Other countries differ a lot.)

Sticker price vs. net price

  • Sticker price: the published cost, including tuition, housing, food and books.
  • Net price: what you actually pay after grants and scholarships. Every school has a net price calculator on its website.

Ways to pay, from best to worst

  • Grants and scholarships: free money you don't repay. Apply for as many as you can.
  • Savings and work: money you already have or earn, including work-study.
  • Federal student loans: usually lower rates and more protections than private loans. In the US you apply with the FAFSA.
  • Private loans: the last resort. Rates can be higher and protections weaker.

Loans cost interest. Some loans start collecting interest while you're still in school, so the balance can be bigger on graduation day than what you borrowed.

Smart moves

  • Compare net prices, not stickers.
  • Consider starting at a community college or choosing an in-state school to cut the bill.
  • Look at trade programs and apprenticeships: many pay you while you learn.
  • Borrow only what you need, and know your monthly payment before you sign.
  • Ask what graduates of the program actually earn.
