Financial Literacy · Lesson 8

Time, inflation and compounding

About 14 minutes · 5 activities · Your answers and progress are saved to your account

Two quiet forces shape every dollar you own. One shrinks it: inflation. The other grows it: compounding. Understanding both, and how much time matters, is one of the biggest advantages a teenager can have.

Inflation: why money buys less over time

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. When prices go up, each dollar buys less. This is called a loss of purchasing power.

For example, with 3% inflation a year, something that costs $10 today costs about $13.44 in ten years. Your $10 hasn't changed, but it now buys about three-quarters of what it used to.

That's why money sitting in a drawer, or in an account that pays almost no interest, slowly loses value. Savings need to earn at least about as much as prices rise, or they shrink in real terms.

Purchasing power (noun)

How much you can buy with a given amount of money. Inflation lowers it; earning a return on your money can protect or increase it.

Compound interest: interest on your interest

With simple interest you earn only on your original amount. With compound interest you earn on your original amount and on the interest you've already earned. That makes growth start slowly, then speed up, like a snowball getting bigger as it rolls.

A handy shortcut is the Rule of 72: divide 72 by the yearly return to estimate how many years it takes to double. At 6% a year, money doubles in about 12 years (72 ÷ 6). At 3%, about 24 years.

Time is the secret ingredient

The most powerful lever isn't a big amount; it's starting early. Someone who invests a little each month for 40 years can end up with far more than someone who starts 10 years later, even if the second person saves more each month. The early money has more time to compound.

You can't control inflation or the market. You can control when you start and how steadily you save.

Key words from this lesson

Inflation
A general rise in prices over time.
Purchasing power
How much your money can buy.
Compound interest
Interest on both your original amount and earlier interest.
Rule of 72
72 divided by the yearly return ≈ years to double.
Return
The gain or loss on an investment.
Time horizon
How long you plan to keep money invested.
